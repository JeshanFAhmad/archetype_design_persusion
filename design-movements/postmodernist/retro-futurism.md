## Overview

Retro-Futurism is design that looks forward through an old lens. It borrows the way earlier generations pictured the future (rocket cars, domed cities, chrome robots, holidays on the Moon) and uses that imagery either sincerely, as a source of optimism, or with a knowing wink at how wrong those predictions turned out.

The raw material comes mostly from the first two-thirds of the twentieth century: pulp science fiction, world's fair pavilions, streamlined industrial design, space-age advertising, and roadside architecture. Southern California's "Googie" coffee shops are a good example of the source style. The Los Angeles Conservancy describes the Googie coffee shops of the architects Armet & Davis as using "space-age decorative elements, dramatic rooflines, glass walls, and bright signage to attract passing motorists," in a city that "aspired to be the city of the future" ([LA Conservancy, Johnie's Coffee Shop](https://www.laconservancy.org/locations/johnies-coffee-shop)). Its listing for Norms La Cienega (1957) calls that building "among the most exuberant and exaggerated Googie designs in the nation" ([LA Conservancy, Norms](https://www.laconservancy.org/locations/norms-la-cienega)).

What makes Retro-Futurism more than nostalgia is the gap between those visions and what actually happened. We got smartphones instead of jetpacks, and climate anxiety instead of cities in the clouds. By the 1980s, past predictions had become a subject in their own right. In 1984 the Smithsonian opened *Yesterday's Tomorrows: Past Visions of America's Future* at the National Museum of American History, displaying more than 300 objects "to show how people in the past have predicted the future" ([Smithsonian Torch, "August 9, 1984"](https://torch.si.edu/2010/08/august-9-1984)). The artist Lloyd Dunn, who started the small-press magazine *PhotoStatic* in 1983, also published one titled *Retrofuturism* ([Wikipedia, "Lloyd Dunn"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd_Dunn)).

That timing places Retro-Futurism inside the broader postmodern turn. Postmodernism is a contested label. The curators of the V&A's 2011 survey *Postmodernism: Style and Subversion 1970–1990* called it "a contentious and theoretically complex topic" ([RCA Research Repository](https://researchonline.rca.ac.uk/1431/)). One recurring feature, though, was a willingness to quote the past instead of rejecting it. A review of that exhibition points to Hans Hollein's facade for the 1980 Venice architecture biennale, which was titled *The Presence of the Past*, and names "appropriation and assemblage" as "a key aesthetic strategy in postmodernism" ([Celia White, Studio International](https://www.studiointernational.com/index.php/from-neon-to-new-order-postmodernism-style-and-subversion-1970-1990)). Modernism promised a clean break with history. Retro-Futurism treats an old idea of the future as one more historical style to borrow, remix, and sometimes question.

## Key Characteristics

**A specific past future.** The core of the style is a borrowed point of view: the future as some earlier moment imagined it. That might be the 1930s streamlined future, the 1950s and 1960s atomic and space-age future, or the 1980s neon-and-computer-grid future. Each has its own visual vocabulary, and good Retro-Futurist work usually picks one rather than mixing them all.

**Optimism, irony, or unease.** Much Retro-Futurism carries the confidence of its sources, a belief that technology would make life easier and more glamorous. Some of it plays that confidence for laughs. Some turns it darker, showing gleaming old visions decayed or gone wrong. Recognizing which tone a design is going for tells you most of what it is trying to say.

**Speculative technology rendered in old materials.** Rockets have fins, robots have rivets, and computers have dials and glowing tubes. The technology is imagined, but its look is built from the materials and manufacturing of the era that imagined it: chrome, Bakelite, enamel, neon, brushed aluminum.

**Space-age and streamlined geometry.** Boomerangs, starbursts, atomic orbits, parabolas, swooping roofs, and teardrop shapes recur. Googie architecture's angled rooflines and dramatic signage, as described by the LA Conservancy, are a built example. The shapes suggest speed and lift even when they sit on a sign or a lamp.

**Display typography.** Lettering is usually expressive: Art Deco capitals with exaggerated proportions, script logos with swooping tails, wide geometric sans-serifs, or in the 1980s strand, chrome and neon effects. The typography often does as much as the imagery to place a design in time.

**Color tied to print and light.** Mid-century travel posters and pulp covers were printed with a few flat inks, so Retro-Futurist palettes often use limited, saturated colors with simple gradients: sunset oranges, teal, cream, deep maroon. The neon-grid strand swaps those for magenta, cyan, and violet glowing on black.

**Deliberately mixed time periods.** This is the clearest marker. A 1930s poster style advertising a trip to a planet discovered in the 2010s. A modern app styled like a 1960s mission-control panel. The mismatch is the point.

**How it differs from "vintage."** A vintage aesthetic recreates how the past looked to itself: a 1950s diner menu, a 1970s record sleeve. Retro-Futurism recreates how the past imagined what came next, or uses old styles to depict genuinely new things. A mid-century font on a coffee shop sign is vintage. The same font announcing tours of a planet orbiting two suns is Retro-Futurist.

## Brand or Website Example

**[NASA/JPL "Visions of the Future" posters](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/galleries/visions-of-the-future/)**

![Travel poster titled "Relax on Kepler-16b." A lone astronaut stands on a reddish plain between rock walls, casting two shadows toward the viewer, while an orange sun and a larger white sun hang in a yellow sky above purple mountains. The tagline reads "The land of two suns: where your shadow always has company." The poster uses Art Deco-style lettering and flat, layered color in the manner of mid-century travel posters to advertise a planet discovered by a modern space telescope, which is the mix of eras at the center of Retro-Futurism.](https://d2pn8kiwq2w21t.cloudfront.net/images/kepler16b.width-1024.jpg)

*"Kepler-16b" from the Visions of the Future series. Courtesy NASA/JPL-Caltech. [Source page](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/kepler-16b-jpl-travel-poster). JPL's [image use policy](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/jpl-image-use-policy/) permits use of its images for any purpose without prior permission, with that credit line, except for NASA and JPL logos and certain other special cases.*

The Jet Propulsion Laboratory's creative team, which it calls "The Studio," produced a series of illustrated travel posters for real planets and moons, presented on the site as "JPL's Exoplanet Travel Bureau presents: Visions of the Future." According to JPL, nine artists, designers, and illustrators made the original 14 posters in consultation with JPL scientists and engineers. The gallery page shows the posters in a grid, each with a short caption, and offers full-size files that JPL says "are free for you to print" at 20 by 30 inches.

Unusually, the designers explain their thinking on the same page, so the Retro-Futurist reading does not have to be guessed.

**An old format for new places.** Creative strategist David Delgado says the team "gravitated to the style of the old posters the WPA created for the national parks," adding, "There's a nostalgia for that era that just feels good." Illustrator Joby Harris explains why the format fits: those posters were made "at a time when color photography was not very advanced," to capture far-off places "from a human perspective," and these planets likewise "haven't been photographed on a human scale yet." The style is chosen for a reason, not just for charm.

**A stated "retro-future" goal.** Typographer Lois Kim says, "We wanted to create a retro-future feel, so we didn't adhere exactly to the period styles, but they definitely informed the design." That sentence describes the movement neatly: period styles as a starting point, adapted rather than copied.

**Specific period references.** The page names them. The Grand Tour poster, about the outer-planet route the Voyager 2 spacecraft flew, came from "transparency overlays from the 1960s." For a starless rogue planet advertised with the tagline "where the nightlife never ends," Harris says the team "leaned heavily on 1930s art deco," calling the result "sort of retro-future fantasy." The Ceres poster's big sign was inspired by Reno's arch reading "the biggest little city in the world."

**Real science inside the fantasy.** Delgado says the team's "unique take was to take one specific thing about the place and focus on the science of it." The Kepler-16b poster above shows two suns and two shadows because the planet orbits a pair of stars, and the scientists' only requested change was to make the two stars different colors. The Kepler-186f poster plays on red-tinted plants that might grow under a different star's light, with the line "the grass is always redder on the other side of the fence." This is Retro-Futurism's mixing of eras at its most purposeful: the visual promise of 1930s tourism attached to findings from a twenty-first-century telescope.

**Optimism with a mission.** The gallery's introduction says, "Imagination is our window into the future," and invites viewers to "remember that you can be an architect of the future." The posters take the confident tone of their sources and point it at real exploration.

## Application to Web Design

**Content and voice.** Decide which past future you are borrowing and why. A science museum might use 1960s mission-control optimism to make research feel exciting. A product launch might use 1930s world's-fair grandeur. Copy can borrow the period's voice ("Visit," "Tomorrow's," "Now boarding") in headlines and taglines, as JPL's "Relax on Kepler-16b" does, while body text stays plain and current. Keep claims accurate. JPL's posters work because the fantasy is attached to real facts.

**Page structure and layout.** Poster-like hero sections suit the style: one strong illustration, a large display title, and a short tagline. Below that, return to a conventional, responsive layout for content and actions. A gallery of poster cards, as on JPL's page, is a good pattern because each card can carry the full style while the grid stays predictable. Plan how tall, portrait-format poster art crops or reflows on wide screens and on phones.

**Typography.** Use expressive period lettering only for display text: titles, section headers, logos. Pair it with a highly readable text face for paragraphs, navigation, and forms. If a display face has unusual letterforms, test that headlines are still legible at mobile sizes. Make sure headings remain real HTML text rather than text baked into images, so they can be searched, translated, and read by screen readers.

**Color palettes.** Build palettes from a few flat colors, the way limited-ink posters did, and use gradients sparingly for skies or glows. Sunset gradients and neon colors on black are popular, but light text on mid-tone oranges or neon on dark purple often fails contrast checks. Test every text color against its background using the [WCAG contrast minimum](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html) of 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large text, and add a solid backing panel behind text that sits over illustration.

**Imagery and illustration.** Commission or create original illustration in a chosen period style rather than relying on generic "retro" stock art. Flat, layered shapes, simplified figures, and dramatic perspective suit travel-poster Retro-Futurism. If you reuse historical imagery, check its rights, and credit it. Write alt text that describes both what the image shows and what it is doing on the page.

**Icons, interface elements, and texture.** Interface details can carry the style subtly: buttons shaped like rounded control-panel switches, progress bars styled as gauges, section dividers drawn as orbits, or a faint print-grain texture on illustration panels. Keep standard affordances intact. A dial-style control should still behave like a normal, labeled input, and a button should still look clickable.

**Animation and interaction.** Motion can evoke the period: a slow starfield, a rocket that lifts as the page scrolls, a neon sign flickering on once. Use it at moments that matter, keep it short, and avoid continuous flicker or flashing, which can be uncomfortable or unsafe for some users. Respect the user's reduced-motion setting, as described in the W3C's guidance on [animation from interactions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html), and provide a pause control for any background video or looping animation.

**User experience and accessibility.** The biggest risk is letting the theme slow people down. Navigation labels should be clear ("Plans," "Contact"), not themed puzzles ("Launch Bay," "Transmissions"), unless the plain meaning is also shown. Forms, prices, and error messages should look and read like a modern, trustworthy interface. Test on small screens, where large decorative headlines and detailed illustration can crowd out content. Used this way, Retro-Futurism gives a site personality and a point of view, while visitors still get what they came for.

[Guide index](README.md) · [Contribution workflow](../CONTRIBUTING.md)

This repository currently contains Markdown research pages. The workflow below helps explain design intent and the connection between research and a proposed website. It does not require building a new website. The instructor also asks for a personal archetype page, one sample per person, and then the completed guide; see [current requirements](CURRENT_REQUIREMENTS.md).

## 1. Capture the brief before drafting

Start your personal brief with the intended audience, the visitor's main task, the feeling you want to create, a brand or website context, and constraints such as device size or existing branding. A hypothetical brand is fine if clearly labeled hypothetical. Do not claim it is a real client.

Record decisions as **proposed**, **confirmed by the contributor**, or **open**. Your AI should summarize your answers and wait for your confirmation. Unknown preferences stay unknown.

## 2. Research each assigned topic

For a persuasion method, explain the decision mechanism, a specific application, and a truthful use of the technique. For an archetype, explain motivation, personality, voice, and the intended audience relationship. For a movement, establish the term's historical scope, distinguishing visual characteristics, and relevant references.

Use a credible source appropriate to the claim: original scholarship for a persuasion explanation, museum or design-history material for a movement, and the actual brand website for an observed interface example. A brand's website alone does not prove its historical influence or official archetype. Label your analysis as interpretation when necessary.

Keep a source ledger with the page title, author or organization, direct URL, claim supported, and date you actually accessed it. Open and read a source before citing it. Do not fill access dates or quotations from guesses. If your AI cannot browse, ask it for research questions rather than invented citations.

## 3. Connect intent to a concrete design

For each topic, complete a short decision table:

| Decision | Your choice | Why it supports the audience or topic | Evidence or validation still needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main message and action | To decide | To explain | To verify |
| Layout and content hierarchy | To decide | To explain | To verify |
| Typography and reading order | To decide | To explain | To verify |
| Color and contrast | To decide | To explain | To verify |
| Imagery or graphics | To decide | To explain | To verify |
| Tone and sample copy | To decide | To explain | To verify |
| Small-screen behavior | To decide | To explain | To verify |

Specify a real choice, such as which content comes first and what a button should say. Avoid unsupported claims that a color or style always causes a particular emotion. Explain the audience and context behind your choice.

Consider one alternative, explain why you did not choose it, and identify a usability risk. If your example is just a written concept, call its checks planned rather than completed.

## 4. Keep the research page consistent

Use the existing topic page as the starting point:

- **Overview:** define the topic in your own words and establish its scope.
- **When to use it:** identify suitable audiences, purposes, and situations.
- **Historical context (design movements):** explain origins and how the style supports, develops, or challenges modernist ideas.
- **Key characteristics:** explain recognizable qualities with evidence.
- **Brand or website example:** link to a specific example and explain which visible details support your interpretation.
- **Application to web design:** explain audience, intended action, and concrete content or visual decisions. Link to your personal brief if it adds useful rationale.
- **Sources:** support factual claims and credit visual material.

Link back to the relevant topic-folder index as well as preserving the assigned owner and root navigation links. Replace the starter status only after the actual research and review support a new status. Do not change other members' pages as a side effect.

## 5. Document visual material responsibly

The instructor's guide expects relevant images and real-world examples. For each visual you include, record its creator, source URL, permission or license basis, and meaningful alternative text. Prefer material you created or are allowed to use. Keep reusable assets under a clearly named folder and use relative links.

For a proposed website example, describe heading order, readable text, contrast checking, keyboard access, mobile layout, and alternatives to motion when relevant. Record whether each check was performed or is still planned. Do not claim accessibility compliance from a visual impression.

## 6. Review before opening the PR

- Every assigned page has substance rather than TODOs or generic AI filler.
- Facts, interpretations, and design proposals are distinguishable.
- The member can explain the topic and rationale without reading the AI's answer.
- Links open, headings render, and any images have attribution and alternative text.
- Design choices follow the member's confirmed intent.
- Source details are real and support the nearby claims.
- Only intended files appear in the PR; no contact details or private chat excerpts.
- Any required AI-use disclosure is included.
- The PR targets the group repository's `main`, and the contributor shares its link.

A design brief records a decision process; it is not evidence that the corresponding page is finished. Use [PROGRESS.md](../PROGRESS.md) for submission and review status.
